    18.1 · Hierarchy of Needs and Automatic Thinking

    How People Prioritize and Decide

    Maslow’s Hierarchy (1943). Physiological needs → Safety → Belongingness → Self-esteem → Self-actualization. Not all needs carry equal weight in decision-making. Click a level to see how it shows up in environmental choices.
    Physiological needs

    Survival comes first

    About 2.1 billion people, roughly a quarter of humanity, still cook over open fires or inefficient stoves. The fuel is what they can get today, even though the smoke harms their health and the forests around them.

    WHO, Household air pollution fact sheet (2025)

    Safety needs

    Security crowds out the long term

    When a state’s unemployment rate rises, its residents search less for “global warming” and become less likely to say it is happening. Jobs feel urgent; the climate can wait.

    Kahn & Kotchen, NBER Working Paper 16241 (2010)

    Belongingness needs

    We do what our neighbors do

    Rooftop solar spreads street by street: each new installation that people can see makes their neighbors more likely to install panels too.

    Bollinger & Gillingham, Marketing Science (2012)

    Self-esteem needs

    Green can be a status symbol

    Hybrid buyers paid extra for a car whose shape announces that it is green, especially where green is admired. Economists call it “conspicuous conservation.”

    Sexton & Sexton, J. Environ. Econ. Manage. (2014)

    Self-actualization needs

    Living out your values

    On the first Earth Day, in 1970, an estimated 20 million Americans joined rallies, marches and teach-ins for environmental reform.

    Earth Day Network; EPA history

    Automatic thinking: instantaneous cognitive responses distinct from slower, conscious, reflective reasoning. Drives most daily decisions effortlessly.

    You felt it a moment ago: the bat and the ball.

    Status quo bias: people prefer their current situation and resist change, even when change would benefit them.

    Whatever is already pre-set, on a form or in a habit, is what most people keep.

    Loss aversion (Kahneman): people prefer avoiding losing what they have more than acquiring an equivalent gain: a powerful driver of inertia.

    18.1 · A Small Experiment

    A Small Experiment

    Your instructor is about to hand something out. Keep this slide open on your laptop.

    Take a moment with it first.

    Any amount from $0 to $50. Answer honestly: there is no right price.

    Locked in
    $0.00

    What happened

    Economists gave half the students in a Cornell class a university coffee mug. Then they asked the mug owners for the lowest price they would sell it for, and asked the students without one the most they would pay. Owners asked about $7. Buyers offered about $3. Same mug, same room, a few minutes of ownership.

    Why

    Loss aversion. Once the mug was theirs, giving it up felt like a loss, and a loss weighs roughly twice as much as an equal gain. So owners needed more money to part with it than buyers would spend to get it. Economists call the gap the endowment effect.

    Why it matters
    • We overvalue what we already have: the car we drive, the gas stove, the lawn, the habit. A greener option has to beat the feeling of giving something up, not just the price tag.
    • That is status quo bias at work. It slows change even when the new option is clearly better.
    • The lesson for anyone trying to change behavior: describe the change as a gain, not a loss, and make the better option the one people already have (the default).

    Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1990). Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem. Journal of Political Economy, 98(6), 1325–1348. Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1992). Journal of Risk and Uncertainty, 5, 297–323 (losses weigh about 2.25 times as much as equal gains).

    18.1 · Social Acceptance and Conformity

    The Pull of the Group

    • Social norms are expectations or standards for behavior widely shared within a group. Our need to belong pushes us to conform, even when we disagree.
    • Asch experiment (1955): 1/3+ of volunteers gave a clearly wrong answer about line lengths to conform with two confederates, demonstrating the power of conformity.
    • Consumer goods: what we wear, what we drive, what we eat. All carry social meaning beyond function, shaped by conformity to group norms.
    What we buy

    Clothing production roughly doubled in fifteen years as fashion turned faster and trends spread through our social circles (Ellen MacArthur Foundation, 2017).

    Inside Asch’s room: try each condition
    <1%of answers wrong

    With no one else answering, people almost never got the lines wrong.

    Rarelywent along

    Facing a single person who gave the wrong answer, people kept their independence.

    35.7%of answers conformed

    With three or more people all giving the same wrong answer, about one answer in three went along with them. Adding more people did not add more pressure.

    5%went along

    When just one other person gave the right answer, the spell broke: only about 5% kept going along with the majority.

    Asch, S. E. (1955). Opinions and social pressure. Scientific American, 193(5), 31–35; Asch (1956). Across 12 critical trials, 74% of participants went along at least once.

    18.1 · What Do You Believe? Bias and Planned Obsolescence

    Belief Perseverance and Built-In Disposal

    • Belief perseverance: clinging to existing beliefs even when presented with evidence to the contrary, common in climate change communication.
    • Confirmation bias: selecting information that confirms existing beliefs and fitting new information into existing frameworks.
    • Planned obsolescence: consumer goods designed to become outdated, requiring consumers to purchase upgrades: a structural driver of waste.
    • EU policy: addressing the creation of more responsible consumer products. Some companies are exploring rental/lease models with built-in upgrades.

    18.2 · The Knowledge-Action Gap

    Why Knowing Is Not Enough

    • Smith College study (1970s): 94% of students identified litter as a concern and said everyone should pick it up, yet only 2% picked up any litter they walked past on the way to the interview.
    • There is often a significant gap between what people know and care about, and what they actually do..
    • Simply providing information is not sufficient to change behavior. Structural nudges, prompts, commitments, and social incentives are more effective.
    • Understanding this gap is the first step: designing for how people actually behave, not how they say they will behave.

    Prompts: A conspicuous reminder that targets behavior at the moment of decision. Based on the principle that an attitude influences behavior more when it can be quickly recalled.

    Energy conservation and recycling improve measurably when prompts are present: signs at recycling bins, reminders on devices.

    Feedback: A prompt containing specific information about the impact of an individual’s behavior; more powerful than a generic reminder.

    Dashboard fuel economy displays: show real-time mpg as you drive. Drivers with these displays achieve several mpg better fuel economy.

    Smart thermostats: real-time home energy consumption data change heating and cooling behavior.

    Home energy reports that compare a household with its neighbors cut electricity use by about 2% on average across hundreds of thousands of homes (Allcott, 2011).

    18.2 · Commitments and Cognitive Dissonance

    Aligning Attitudes and Actions

    72%reduction in idling among drivers who displayed the sticker
    • Canada anti-idling study: drivers who displayed an ‘I turn my engine off when parked’ sticker showed a 72% reduction in idling..
    • Cognitive dissonance: the unpleasant tension we feel when our attitudes and actions do not match, e.g., caring about the environment but leaving car idling.
    • Commitments reduce dissonance by aligning behavior with stated values, especially when made publicly.

    18.4 · How Important Are the Words We Use?

    A Difficult Decision

    Read the briefing, then choose a program.

    Briefing

    Imagine the country is preparing for the outbreak of a new disease, which is expected to kill 600 people. Two programs to fight it have been proposed. Assume the estimates of their consequences are exact.

    Locked in: . Watch the screen at the front.
    Some of you read this

    A: 200 people will be saved.
    B: a 1-in-3 chance all 600 are saved.

    0% chose the sure thing (A)
    Original study: 72%
    The rest of you read this

    A: 400 people will die.
    B: a 1-in-3 chance nobody dies.

    0% chose the sure thing (A)
    Original study: 22%

    Same programs, same numbers: 200 saved out of 600 is 400 dead. Described as lives saved, most people choose the sure thing. Described as deaths, most gamble. The frame changed the choice.

    Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1981). The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice. Science, 211(4481), 453–458. Wording adapted (a “new disease”).

    18.4 · How Do Frames Work?

    Zooming In vs. Zooming Out

    • A photographer zooming in or out on a city near zebra habitat in Nairobi gives completely different impressions of the human-wildlife relationship. The same principle applies to words.
    • Words set similar boundaries: the way a situation is described in words frames how people understand and interpret it.

    Chapter 18 · A Framework for Environmental Behavior Change

    A Framework for Environmental Behavior Change

    From Knowing to Acting.Apply it to a case from today:
    1
    Understand the Decision:
    Who is making the decision? What needs, biases, and social norms are at work?
    2
    Identify the Barriers:
    Loss aversion? Perceived inconvenience? Status quo bias? Emotional defense mechanisms?
    3
    Design the Intervention:
    Use prompts, feedback, commitments, nudges, and incentives, tailored to the specific barrier.
    4
    Frame the Message:
    Choose words and images that activate the right associations: positive solutions, not just problems.
    5
    Make Change Visible:
    Social norms drive conformity. Make sustainable behaviors the visible, normal choice.
